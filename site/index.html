Act II

The Cost Of Not Guessing

Act II

The Cost Of Not Guessing

There is no clock on the table, so nothing has to be fast. Where a street can be counted, the engine counts it — every outcome, not a sample of them — and the answer comes back exact. The bill arrives in seconds.

  1. 01 The river 990 dealer hands All five board cards are known, so the only unknown is the dealer's two. Every one of the 990 possible holdings is graded and counted. The answer is a tally, not an estimate. 9 ms
  2. 02 The flop 1,070,190 outcomes Three board cards known. That leaves 1,081 turn-and-river runouts, each one graded against all 990 dealer hands — 1,081 × 990, every pair, exactly once. The spec suggested sampling here. There is no timer, so there is no reason to. ~9 s
  3. 03 Pre-flop 169 hand classes Nothing to enumerate: the exact count runs to about 100 million runouts per holding, against 1,326 holdings. So it is measured instead — 2,000,000 runouts and 6,000 boards for each of the 169 classes, once, offline. Afterwards a decision costs nothing at all: it is a dictionary lookup. ≈4 h · paid once

The faster wrong answer

There is a way to write the flop enumeration that runs in 2.7 seconds, where the exact one takes about nine. It hands the dealer the two lowest cards of every draw and the board the two highest, and it visits a sixth of the outcome space. Nothing about it looked broken. It reported a 55% push rate on a hand whose true push rate is 14.5%, and it was caught only by cross-checking against an independent Monte Carlo run — which is now a permanent test, so it stays caught.

The conventional answer

Poker players have always called 7♥2♠ the worst hand in the game. The engine's own table disagrees, twice over. 2♣3♦ — the hand this page opened with — wins less often: 29.2% of the time, against 31.7% for the seven-deuce. But 7♥2♠ is worth less: −0.96 ante units against −0.93. There are two defensible answers to the question of the worst hand in poker, and they are not the same hand.

Act IV

1 In 156

Act IV

1 In 156

A single hand swings the bankroll by about 4.2 units. The engine's edge is 0.027. That is one part signal to a hundred and fifty-six parts noise, and it means that over any stretch of hands you will actually sit through, the result is noise wearing the costume of a result.

One of those hairlines is the edge. The other 155 are the swing around it, and nothing about a session's profit or loss tells you which one you were watching.

The edge, measured

−0.0270

Units per hand, ± 0.0076, over 35,000 hands. This is the number to believe, and no single run produces it.

  • 40 antes 97%

    50,000 chips — the stack the project actually simulated. Ten sessions of 2,000 hands: 8 of the 10 bankrolls went broke. The 10,000-hand run died at hand 81. At this size the stack cannot cover its own expected loss, which is 270 antes.

  • 200 antes 81%
  • 400 antes 57%
  • 800 antes 17%

    Even here it is not safe: the deep run, at 800 antes, went broke at hand 4,990 — a 2% draw, which is to say an unlucky one. One trial settles nothing, and this is the act that says so.

  • 1,200 antes 2%

Ruin over 10,000 hands, from the diffusion model at μ = 0.027 and σ = 4.2 — the same two numbers the rail above is built from. The stack has to be in the hundreds of antes before survival is the likely outcome, and a 5,000-chip maximum bet against a 50,000-chip stack is nowhere near it.

What the tool said

The simulator keeps playing past zero on purpose — truncating a run at ruin would drop exactly the worst hands out of the sample. The cost is that a broke bankroll's closing figure means nothing, and the summary line prints it anyway: the run above once closed with “profitable trials 7 of 10” when 8 of the 10 bankrolls had gone broke. That bug is still unfixed. It is in the README, listed beside the other two, and it is why the paragraph above quotes the ruin hand and the Rao-Blackwellised estimate rather than the bankroll line.

The Verdict

Four estimates of one quantity — how much this engine loses per hand — each measured to a different precision. They disagree with each other by more than the thing being measured, which is the honest state of the evidence and the reason the page has one more figure to show.

published optimum −2.185% 0 −2 −4 −6

The four rows are the same engine playing the same game, and no two of them agree. The 10,000-hand run lost four and a half times the true edge; the 20,000-hand run made money. Weighted by the inverse of each run's own variance, they collapse to a single estimate with an interval narrow enough to be worth something — and that interval lands 0.68 standard errors from the published optimum of −2.185%. That is a statement about how tightly the engine has been measured, not about how well it plays. Both halves of it are true, and the page has no interest in only the flattering one.

It does not beat the house.

Nothing does. Ultimate Texas Hold'em returns about −2.185% of the ante to a player who plays it perfectly, and that number is a property of the rules, not of the cards or of this program. So optimal play here means losing the least — and losing the least, measured to within a fraction of a standard error, is the entire job a decision engine can do. Every figure on this page that read like a failure was the engine doing that job. The hand it opened with won nothing and was still the right bet. The 55% that was really 14.5%. The run that went broke at hand 81. The summary line that called eight ruined bankrolls profitable. None of them are the engine being wrong about poker. All of them are the same thing: the engine refusing to guess, and the arithmetic refusing to flatter.
